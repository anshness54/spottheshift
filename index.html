Evolutionary Mechanisms

Evolution is defined quantitatively as a change in allele frequencies within a population over time. It is not an individual organism adapting, but an entire population shifting across generations. Urban environments introduce extreme, novel selection pressures that rapidly shift these frequencies at unprecedented speeds.

Variation

Genetic mutations constantly provide the raw material. Without variation, there is nothing to select between.

Heritability

Traits must be passed down. Advantageous mutations accumulate across generations.

Selection Pressure

Cities introduce physical hazards (traffic), thermal extremes (asphalt), and chemical toxins.

Time

Species with fast generation times (insects, small rodents, fish) exhibit observable evolution within decades.

The Urban Heat Island Effect

Impervious surfaces (asphalt, concrete, roofing) absorb extreme amounts of solar radiation, while the removal of vegetation eliminates natural evaporative cooling. This creates localized temperature anomalies 3–8°C higher than surrounding rural baselines, driving immediate thermal selection.

THERMAL MAXIMUM ALERT
Insect species, such as the Acorn Ant, show measurable Critical Thermal Maximum (CTmax) elevation in urban cores. Without this rapid genetic adaptation, populations on urban asphalt would experience total thermal collapse.

Chemical & Structural Pressures

Beyond heat, cities are fundamentally novel environments chemically and structurally. When organisms cannot migrate away from industrial pollutants or sheer glass architecture, they either face extirpation or rapid evolutionary divergence.

CASE STUDY: TOXIC SELECTION
Atlantic Killifish populations in the Elizabeth River estuary have evolved an AHR-pathway mutation granting up to 8,000× resistance to lethal polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) compared to baseline rural populations.
